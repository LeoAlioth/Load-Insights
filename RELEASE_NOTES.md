# Release Notes

## 0.5.1

### Fixes

- **A car charger is measured in amps per phase, not in watts.** That is what the standard limits and what a charger is actually set to: 6 A is the floor in IEC 61851 (a Tesla will go to 5), 32 A is the common ceiling, and 63 A on three phases or about 80 A on one are the extremes. A band on total watts described none of it - it called a three-phase charger idling at its 6 A minimum a 4.1 kW load and scored it as large, while the same 4.1 kW on one phase is 18 A and quite a different thing. The old band also stopped at 11.5 kW, so a 22 kW wallbox at 32 A three-phase - entirely ordinary - scored barely at all.
- **The biggest resistive loads are heating elements again.** The band stopped at 9 kW, which ruled out an electric boiler or the backup heat in a heat pump's air handler; both are 10 to 20 kW and nothing else about them is unusual. Its duration ceiling moves with it, but only as far as two hours: a heating element and a car charging share a power factor and duration is most of what separates them, so buying room for whole-house heat costs exactly the discrimination that matters. Past two hours the two are offered as alternatives, and a meter with a name settles it outright.
- **A whole induction hob is not one ring.** Cooking stopped at 3.5 kW; in Europe a hob is commonly wired across two phases and peaks around 7 kW.
- **Programmes may run far longer than they were allowed.** A dishwasher's eco cycle reaches four hours where the profile stopped at two and a half, and a washer-dryer combination runs a washing and a drying programme back to back - eight hours is not unusual, against a ceiling of one hour forty. Widening them makes the two overlap, and the margin test then declines to choose rather than guessing, which is the honest answer where the meter has no name to settle it.
- **Where a family reaches further than the appliances beneath it, that is deliberate** - a 150 W towel rail and a 15 kW boiler are both heating elements and neither is a hot water tank, so they are named as the family and nothing more. Now said in the README, since the numbers no longer look like an oversight.

## 0.5.0

### New Features

- **A load on a named meter is told by that name, not by its shape.** When a load turns out to sit on one of your device meters, whatever you called that meter now decides what it is - and it is stated without the question mark the shape-based guesses carry. This is the best evidence the integration ever gets and it went unused: shape can only say a load draws 1.8 kW at a heating element's power factor, while whoever wired the site already wrote `Boiler` on the meter. It wins in exactly the cases worth having, both of them real: a boiler that cycles for 70 seconds looks nothing like the quarter of an hour a hot water tank is expected to run, and a pressure pump behind a variable-speed drive corrects its own power factor to 0.96 and reads as a heating element. Matched on whole words, against the friendly name and the entity id both, in English and Slovene - `Water Pump`, `Hidrofor` and `sensor.kotlovnica_well_pump_energy` all say pump. **Device words only**: most meters are named after rooms, and a room says nothing about what is plugged into it. And a name explains a reading rather than excusing one that disagrees, so a 40 W load on a meter called `EVSE` is still not a car charging.
- **The README lists what the classifier can say**, as the two layers it actually has - the physics stated plainly, and beneath each family the specific appliances it may be asked about, with the readings each rests on.

## 0.4.0

### New Features

- **And when it generally runs.** A row now also says *evenings*, *overnight*, *daytime*, *weekdays*, *weekends* - where the load actually keeps to a time. Unlike the appliance guess this is a measurement rather than a prior: "runs in the evening" is a fact about your house, not a belief about houses, so it is stated plainly instead of asked as a question. It stays quiet where a load keeps no time worth mentioning, which is most of them - a phrase on every row would distinguish nothing - and it will not claim a time on the strength of a single occasion, since every run inside one evening falls in the same hours by construction. The week is judged per DAY rather than per group, because there are five weekdays and two weekend days, so a load running uniformly puts 71 % of its energy midweek and would otherwise read as a weekday load. Where the words cover the week the seven-character sparkline gives up its place on the row, the two being the same fact.
- **The naming page says what is running now, and when each load last ran.** Everything on that page described a load in the abstract - what it draws, how long for, how often, what it might be - and left out the one thing the person reading it actually has to go on: their own memory of the last hour. They know the dishwasher went on after dinner and that nothing has run in the workshop since Tuesday. A row now ends with **running now**, **just finished**, or **last ran 15 min ago**, which turns naming a load from a puzzle into walking over and looking at what is on. A load is recognised as running from the moment it starts, by the size of the step, so it does not have to finish first - though the very first time a load ever runs there is nothing to compare it with, because a signature is a step up paired with the step down that undoes it. The *Detected loads* sensor carries `running` and `last_seen_ago_s` per signature for anyone building their own card.

## 0.3.1

### Fixes

- **A named load's meter no longer steps down when the library is rebuilt.** 0.3.0 gave a name back to the load that wore it, but the rebuilt library only covers ten days where the old one had been accumulating since the day it was installed - so the name returned attached to far less energy than its meter had already published, and Home Assistant read the drop as a meter reset. Anze's compressor went from 18.98 kWh to 7.30. The old reading is now carried across as a **floor** rather than something to add: the two periods overlap, so adding them would count those ten days twice, while a floor simply stops the meter going backwards and ceases to matter of its own accord once the rebuilt library has accumulated past it. It is kept per name rather than on the signature, so `per_run_wh` and the hour and weekday charts still describe the load rather than its lifetime - and it holds the reading steady even while a name is still waiting for its load to turn up again.

## 0.3.0

### New Features

- **A name outlives the library it was written on.** Naming a load is the one thing in the signature library that a person put there by hand - it creates a device, an energy meter and a place on the Energy dashboard - and everything else in that library is re-learned from ten days of history in a few minutes. Until now a reset took the names with it, and so would any future update that changed how loads are found: the library is keyed on a detector generation, and when that moves the whole store is discarded behind a line in the log. On every installation at once, the first time it happened. Names are now carried across both - the deliberate reset and the silent one - as descriptions of what wore them, and handed back to whatever the rebuild finds that looks like the same load, by the same test that decides two signatures are one thing. Where nothing matches, which is exactly what a genuinely changed site looks like, the name does not return; it waits instead, visible in the *Detected loads* sensor's `awaiting_name` attribute rather than disappearing. Because a named load's sensors are keyed on the NAME, a name that comes back brings its own history with it. **Ordinary updates were never affected** - 0.1.0 to 0.2.0 kept every name, because the generation did not move - but nothing guaranteed that, and the guarantee is the point.

## 0.2.0

Load detection learns to say *where*. In 0.1.0 it found what the house does and described it well, but almost nothing ever landed on the device meter that already measured it - at a site with a metered boiler, car charger and two pumps, every one of 173 signatures read as "main". This release is that path made to work, end to end, measured against ten days of two real sites.

### New Features

- **A load is attributed to a device meter by the ENERGY it recorded, not by matching two sessions**: a meter too slow to resolve a load still knows how much energy it logged while that load ran, because sampling error cancels in an integral where it does not in a power reading. A Shelly reporting once a minute gets three samples of a 66-second run and reports half the true power - but the watt-hours over that minute are right. So every main-meter session is now also offered to each device's raw readings and scored by how far the energy ratio sits from one. Measured against a boiler's own meter over ten days, the ratio ran 0.97 at the tenth percentile, 1.02 at the median and 1.07 at the ninetieth, with 465 of 479 sessions inside the band - where matching the two meters' *sessions* managed 38 of 381. It is compared as a **rise**, against the same span just before, so a device that merely happened to be drawing cannot claim a load it had nothing to do with. At the test site the unnamed-load list - the one you work through by hand - fell from 173 entries to 55, because the rest now carry a meter of their own.
- **The smallest change worth noticing is measured, and configurable**: the step threshold was fixed at 100 W, which was the binding constraint on both test sites, whose median sample-to-sample movement is 3 to 5 W. A fridge compressor steps 60 to 150 W and could never clear it, so two fridges at one site were invisible. The floor is now learned per phase from what the reading actually does, rises with whatever is running (a phase carrying five kilowatts wanders by tens of watts where the same phase idle wanders by three), and sits under a **Smallest change to notice** setting on the Detection settings page for when small things clutter the list.
- **Readings are scaled by their unit**: a meter publishing kW is no longer read as watts. A charger doing exactly that had its 2 kW sessions arrive as the number 2, so they never matched the 2000 W the main meter saw and the load stayed unattributed.
- **An inverter carries its own volts and amps**, so a site whose power factor came from pairing one meter's watts with another meter's current now gets a coherent triple from a single circuit. That subtraction artifact had been reading as a power factor.

### Fixes

- **Cross-meter matching takes the best fit, not the first**: the matcher took the first downstream session that passed and spent it, which is order-dependent - a main session that merely fitted consumed the one a closer match needed. Every passing pair is now scored on how far apart the two meters put the moment and the size, and the closest is taken first.
- **How far apart two meters may be about the same instant is measured, not assumed**: fifteen seconds assumed they report together. They do not - one site's inverter publishes every 5.3 s while a Shelly on a device publishes every 52, so a load can be most of a minute old on one before it appears on the other. Each detector already learns its own interval; the two are added, floored at the old tolerance.
- **A session remembers which signature took it.** It used to be looked up in a display list capped at 200 entries, so a backfill slice that filed more than that lost the answer for all but the last few - and with it every location. Backfill is exactly when a load's place should be established.
- **Only the START of a session has to line up.** Demanding both endpoints within a tight window was wrong: on a busy main meter a down-step can pair with a different edge, so the session runs on. Measured at one site, 443 boiler cycles started within a minute of a main-meter session while their ends differed by 7 s at the median and 438 s at the third quartile. Matching now takes the start and a comparable duration, so a kettle inside an hour-long run still cannot claim it.
- **A device meter is given time to answer.** A session was discarded 120 seconds after it closed - a figure meant for two *phases* of one load closing together, which happens within seconds, not for a Shelly getting round to it. A session is not even judgeable until the reading reaches past its end, so detection running once a minute placed a boiler's sightings 1 time in 378 where the backfill placed all of them. Live detection and backfill now agree.
- **A meter that fell silent no longer answers for a window it never saw.** Sample-and-hold carries the last reading forward for as long as it is allowed, and "it drew exactly what it was drawing before" is indistinguishable from a device that really did stay put. Both ends of a window must now be covered by real samples.
- **A signature no longer over-states how tightly it was measured.** Merging two signatures averaged their spreads and threw away the distance between their means, so folding two tight signatures 300 W apart together produced one claiming its sightings sat within a few watts of each other. That figure feeds tightness, which feeds evidence, which feeds the confidence shown on the naming page - so a merge made a signature look *better* measured the further apart the things it merged were. At one site twelve of 216 signatures were reporting a tightness they did not have.
- **The Detected loads sensor no longer tries to put its library in the database.** Its attributes are a live inspection surface - the signature library, what each device meter saw, the last forty sessions - and three of them were excluded from the recorder while the other ten went through. On a real site that is past Home Assistant's 16 KB ceiling, so HA logged a warning saying the database would suffer and then stored nothing anyway: 989 times at one house. Every attribute is now excluded; the state, how many unexplained loads are on, is the part with a history worth keeping.
- **Merging can no longer walk.** Consolidation is transitive: every merge re-centres the tolerance on the new mean, so one signature can reach another, the pair a third, and on it goes - a ladder from 400 W down to 25 W collapsed into a single signature calling itself 94 W and describing none of them. Consolidation now re-checks before each merge instead of judging a whole batch against the state it started in, and refuses one that would leave the pool wider than the tolerance that admitted it.

## 0.1.0

### New Features

- **Consumption forecast from the Energy dashboard**: Load Insights reads the sources and individual devices you have already set up in Home Assistant's Energy dashboard - grid import/export, PV, battery, metered devices - and the recorder's hourly statistics for them, and publishes a consumption forecast: expected power over the coming hour, today's and tomorrow's kWh, and a seven-day hourly series in the same shape the PV forecast integrations use, so one dashboard card can plot both. A fourth sensor forecasts the **unmetered remainder** - consumption minus every individually metered device - which is what nothing else on the site measures. Recomputed every hour from twelve weeks of history; nothing to configure beyond a name. Public holidays are treated as Sundays, in the history the profile learns from and in the week it forecasts, wherever Home Assistant's Workday integration is set up (its `holidays` library is what supplies them). A device added to the dashboard recently does not shorten the remainder's history: before its first statistic it counts as zero, since its energy was unmetered - and therefore part of the remainder - until then; the sensor's `remainder_complete_since` attribute says from when every device is covered.
- **A forecast per metered device**: every device the Energy dashboard lists gets its own forecast sensor in the same shape, ready to use. A device added to the dashboard later appears after a reload.
- **Actual beside forecast**: every forecast sensor also carries `history`, the last 48 hours as they actually happened, in the same shape as `detailedForecast` - so one entity feeds both halves of an actual-vs-forecast chart (a Plotly Graph Card example is in the README).
- **Download diagnostics**: one file with every forecast's fit, what the Energy dashboard said, the scoring ledger and load detection's whole library - the thing to attach to a question about a number, instead of a screenshot of each attribute.
- **It says when something is half-done**: a weather entity without a temperature sensor (or the reverse), a PV site with no forecast linked, a metered device that publishes only energy - each raised as a repair notification while it is true, and withdrawn when it is fixed. None of them is an error, which is exactly why they used to go unnoticed.
- **Two services**: `refresh` recomputes now instead of on the hour, for checking whether a change took effect; `reset_detection` forgets the signature library and starts the backfill again.
- **Icons, and the site device lands in the grid meter's area.**
- **One sensor per thing forecast, and a metered device's forecast lives on that device**: the state is the prediction for the coming hour and the attributes carry the rest, so daily totals, import and export figures and the scores are attributes rather than entities of their own - a template sensor makes one wherever you want it. A device's forecast is attached to the Home Assistant device behind its Energy dashboard entry, so the boiler's forecast appears on the boiler; only loads found by profiling the meter, which have no device of their own, get a new one.
- **What the meter will do, not just what the house needs**: a grid forecast - consumption less the PV your Energy dashboard's own forecast integrations predict, with the battery simulated in between - published as the expected meter power for the coming hour, tomorrow's import and export in kWh, and the pack's state of charge hour by hour. The battery model follows the house rather than any charge policy, and says so; without an SOC or a capacity on the dashboard it is skipped and the net reported before the battery.
- **Base load**: what the site draws with nothing switched on, per phase and per meter - the idle floor load detection tracks anyway. Worth watching for creep.
- **Load detection, first stage**: give Load Insights your grid connection and your inverters (Configure -> Grid connection, Inverters) and it works out what the house draws - the meter, plus what each inverter puts out less what it takes in - then watches that at the meter's own cadence for loads switching on and off - as sessions with levels, merged across phases, matched into signatures it describes in words: "6.1 kW on A+C, ~80 s, every 3 min, seen 258 times". A load is a STEP UP paired with the step down that undoes it, so loads may overlap freely - the kettle inside the fridge's hour closes on its own - and a slow drift, whether the sun coming up on a grid meter or an element tapering, is never mistaken for one. Where the Energy dashboard has an array, a cloud is recognised as the sun rather than filed as a load switching on. Meters below the main one need no setting up: every device on your Energy dashboard is used automatically, so a load that the main meter and a device's own meter both see is placed on that device - and where the dashboard says one device sits inside another, the innermost one wins. Two new sensors, *Detected loads* and *Unknown load power*. What no device meter accounts for you name once, on its own page - each named load then gets a running and a power sensor, and two given the same name are one device on different settings, which the page suggests when it sees them. Give the meter its per-phase voltage and current (or a power factor sensor) and each load's OWN power factor follows, from how far the watts and the reactive power moved when it switched: with that, every signature is offered a guess at what KIND of thing it is - a heating element, a motor, a three-phase motor, electronics, an appliance that varies its own power, a programme, a car charging - with the readings the guess rests on, two families named where both fit, and never a claim, since shape cannot tell a hair dryer from a fan heater. Each also says where it is by exclusion, "in Hiša, outside Blaževa Soba", or the phase it is on where no meter saw it, and carries three separate confidences: whether it is a real repeating load, what kind it is, and where it lives. Loads seen only once are kept but not offered for naming. Using what is learned in the forecast follows.
- **The house is worked out, not configured**: given the grid connection and the inverters, what the house draws is arithmetic rather than a choice - `house = grid meter + the sum over inverters of (output - input)`. An inverter contributes what it *adds*: a PV string inverter has no AC input and contributes its whole output, a hybrid with the grid flowing through it contributes the difference, so the grid it passed along is not counted twice, and whatever sits between the utility meter and a hybrid's own AC input falls out of the same sum because the hybrid measures that input itself. No wiring flag enters into it - the expression is arrangement-independent, giving the same answer whether a second inverter feeds the main bus or sits on the first one's load port, which is tested both ways. Checked against a real site over ten days: identical to the per-phase template sensors its owner had built by hand, to the watt, on all three phases. Setup is three pages - **Detection settings** (how often, how sure), **Grid connection** (the meter, and whether a utility, a generator or nothing is behind it), **Inverters** (each one's output, its grid-side input, and where its battery sits). A reading you already have that *is* the house still wins if you set one.
- **Named loads get an energy meter**: naming a load gives it a device with **Energy** in kWh, ready for the Energy dashboard's individual-devices section, and **Average power** - the energy that arrived over the last interval divided by the time it covers. Both count only sessions that finished, so a 44-second load contributes whether or not anyone was looking at the right moment, and the two agree with each other by construction. When a named load's behaviour changes in a step - a different programme, a replaced element - the old fingerprint keeps its history and the naming page offers to move the name onto whatever replaced it, carrying the energy across so the meter never reads backwards.
- **Guesses at the appliance, not just the physics**: beneath the family, a second and deliberately weaker layer names a specific thing where several readings agree - a three-phase workshop machine, a pump, a hot water tank, cooking, a dishwasher, a washing machine, a dryer, a fridge. Always as a question, never a claim, since "runs at six in the evening" is a prior about a house rather than a measurement. A **three-phase motor** is its own family rather than a guess: a balanced load on all three legs at a motor's power factor is a measurement, and almost nothing else in a house does it. A new reading makes several of these possible - how far a load **wanders inside its own run**. An element holds its level; anything behind a variable-speed drive glides without ever taking a step, so a pressure pump running 104 to 247 W read as one flat level at a near-unity power factor, which is to say a small heater. The row now says what it varies between, in watts. A load that keeps a clock says how often, because "70 s every 14 min" is what its owner recognises first.
- **The naming page waits, and then shows only what it is sure of**: while the backfill is still reading history it shows how far along it is and a Refresh, rather than rows from a library holding whatever happened in the first few days. After that, a load must have repeated - and repeated tightly - before it is offered, at a threshold you can set. If too few clear the bar the best of the rest come along anyway, so the list never runs dry. Rows are ordered by what a load costs weighted by how recognisable it is: a time it keeps to, a day, a clock, a size worth noticing, a meter that half-saw it.
- **Detection runs every minute** by default, and is configurable. It reads the recorder in one query per pass rather than one per entity, and writes its state hourly rather than every pass, so the faster cadence costs less than the old slow one did.
- **Readings are scaled by their unit**: a meter publishing kW is no longer read as watts. An EV charger doing exactly that had its 2 kW sessions arrive as the number 2, so they never matched the 2000 W the main meter saw and the load stayed unattributed.

- **A device's own state sharpens its next hours**: give a device its state sensor under Configure - the boiler its tank temperature, the pump its level - and its current reading adjusts that device's forecast for the coming six hours, with the spread narrowing where the state explains what happens. Learned from the sensor's recorded past against the device's consumption; sign and size come from the data, and a sensor that turns out not to matter is ignored.
- **Any sensor as an explanatory input**: attach a tariff block, an electricity price, irradiance, an occupancy sensor - anything - and Load Insights works out from its recorded history whether it explains consumption, the same way it does for a calendar, and ignores it when it does not. A sensor with few values is read as it is; a numeric one is cut into bands. Inputs that are a pure function of weekday and hour, tariffs included, will not engage: the weekly profile already knows them.
- **Calendars, without saying what they mean**: link any calendars under Configure and the forecast learns from their past events what each does to consumption - an absence calendar shows up as the house dropping to its base load by day, a bookings calendar as busier evenings - and applies that to the week ahead where their events fall. Event titles are learned on top, only for how they differ from the calendar's average, so one shared calendar with "away" and "guests" entries yields both roles. A calendar that turns out to mean nothing for consumption is ignored, and the `calendars` attribute on every forecast sensor shows what was learned.
- **The forecast scores itself**: what it predicted for each hour a day and a week before is remembered and compared with what happened. Six sensors give the trailing week's mean error and bias for the site and for the unmetered remainder, the hour-ahead error, and yesterday's day-ahead error in kWh; attributes carry the per-lead table and how often the actual fell inside the forecast's spread. Per-device scores are an attribute on each device forecast sensor. The first figures appear after a day, the day-ahead ones after two.
- **The forecast responds to the weather**: link a weather entity (pre-filled when there is exactly one) and an outdoor temperature sensor, at setup or later under Configure, and every forecast learns how much cold and heat add to consumption - from the sensor's recorded past, applied to the weather entity's coming week. It only takes effect where it actually explains consumption; the boiler will show a response, the car charger will not, and each sensor's `temperature_response` attribute shows which.
- **A spread on every forecast hour**: `detailedForecast` rows carry `kwh_p10` and `kwh_p90` beside `kwh` - the weighted 10th and 90th percentiles of that hour-of-week's history, in the PV forecast integrations' naming. An hour where the car charges on some weeks shows the range, not an average that never happens. The forecast attributes are also no longer written to the recorder database on every refresh; they stay live and on cards.
